# Semi-automated crop field drawings

Background material for this app, archived from the studio output [Semi-automated crop field drawings](https://mortensylvest.studio/outputs/QyRMkgmy4AgRHRTrhsql) (catalogue id 2025-SEM-001).

The pen plots were made by tracing field perimeters from aerial photographs in Rhino, then running a Grasshopper script for the double lines and texture. This repository is the browser version of that process: draw a boundary, generate tractor tracks, export an SVG for the plotter.

Captions are as published on the studio page. Photographs are by msn.

## Work

- **Year:** 2025
- **Materials:** Black 0.5 mm penplot on 310g A3 viking Iris 310 watercolour paper
- **Dimensions:** 297 × 420 × 1 mm
- **Edition:** 16 designs, 64 of each
- **Exhibition:** web at mortensylvest.studio (2025)

Semi-automated crop field drawings is a series of 16 penplotter drawings exploring the intersection of agricultural machinery, landscape geometry, and automated production. Each drawing exists in an edition of 64, creating a deliberate dialogue between digital reproduction and the mechanical patterns found in modern farming practices. The drawings originate from aerial photographs of crop fields along the route from Copenhagen to Roskilde, documenting the first 48 kilometers of a longer journey through Denmark's cultivated landscapes. The production process mirrors contemporary farming methods. Using Rhino 3D software, field perimeters are manually traced, while a custom Grasshopper script generates tractor-like patterns across the landscape. This hybrid approach of automated and hand-drawn elements reflects modern agriculture, where GPS-guided tractors work in tandem with human operators. The selected fields come from a zone where urban and rural landscapes merge, capturing the gradual transition from city to countryside. This liminal space reveals how industrial areas, housing developments, and agricultural land interweave, challenging our traditional notion of clear boundaries between urban and rural environments. For urban dwellers, these drawings serve as a reminder of the agricultural landscapes that sustain city life. While our digestive systems engage daily with food from these fields, we rarely experience these spaces physically. The artwork creates a geometric "window" into these distant yet intimately connected landscapes. The numerical choices in the project — 16 designs and 64 editions — reference computer science and digital systems: 16 relates to hexadecimal notation, while 64 refers to 64-bit computing architecture. This digital symbolism connects the artwork to the computational systems that increasingly govern agricultural production. The geometric patterns visible in these fields represent some of the most widespread human-made forms on the planet, created by standardized machinery used globally in industrial agriculture. The artwork captures both the rigid geometry of mechanical production and the organic variations that emerge from the landscape's natural contours. In translating these agricultural geometries into art, the project reveals how technological systems — from tractors to plotters — mediate our relationship with the landscape, creating new forms of beauty while raising questions about standardization, automation, and our changing relationship with the land that feeds us.
